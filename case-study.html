The College Event Lifecycle Study

10 respondents interviewed 6 event categories ITER, Siksha 'O' Anusandhan · B.Tech CSE Submitted 12 March 2026
01

The problem

Ten student organizers, six kinds of events, one recurring answer: nothing gets written down. Every one of them had a plan for the day itself — but ask what happens after the event ends, and the paper trail stops. Two of the ten never produced any formal SOP at all.

And even among the eight who did, the specifics that actually would have helped next year's team — how to sync AV across three halls, what a hybrid check-in workflow looks like, which vendor to call when a sponsor pulls out days before the event — lived only in that year's group chat. Read all ten interviews back to back and the same undocumented categories keep resurfacing: permissions and approval chains, technical setup, and volunteer authority structure. None of it is written anywhere a future organizer could find it.

"Hybrid event technical setup and online participant management are entirely undocumented." — Coordinator, Coding/Hackathon
02

My approach

I interviewed 10 organizers who'd actually run an event in the past year — hackathons, cultural fests, a workshop series, a startup pitch night, a sports meet, a tech symposium — six categories in total. Everyone answered the same structured questionnaire, walking through the full event lifecycle: ideation, team formation, execution, crisis management, stakeholder and permission management, marketing, and the post-event debrief.

For each respondent I scored role clarity, execution, crisis response, and marketing effectiveness on a 5-point scale, and logged their planning lead time, event scale, SOP status, and what actually went wrong on the day. That gave me one master table to compare across very different events — a hackathon and a cultural fest don't share a budget or a venue, but they share the same lifecycle stages.

Event types covered (10 respondents)
03

The sharpest finding

Here's the pattern that only shows up once you line up all ten answers side by side. Every single respondent — 10 of 10 — had a written contingency plan. Eight of ten could point to some kind of formal SOP. On paper, that looks like a well-documented ecosystem.

But ask what's actually inside those documents, and the gap opens up immediately: not one of the ten had written down the specific technical or institutional knowledge that would have actually saved next year's team time — AV sync procedures, hybrid streaming setup, sponsor fallback lists, a volunteer authority chart. Everyone has a checklist. Nobody has the manual.

What's actually documented, out of 10 respondents

Contingency plans and SOPs exist on paper. Granular technical & institutional knowledge — the part a new organizer actually needs — doesn't.

It shows up in the scores too. Technical difficulty averaged 2.7 out of 5 — the lowest metric in the entire study — sitting right next to the one category with zero documentation.

04

My recommendation

Six changes, in the order I'd tackle them if I were handing this to next year's organizing committee.

01

Start planning 6–8 weeks out, minimum

Events planned more than 3 months ahead scored higher on execution and had noticeably fewer day-of crises.

02

Formalize SOPs — and archive one every edition

2 of 10 organizers still had no formal SOP. A written one, filed after every edition, stops the reset each year.

03

Write contingency plans down in real detail

"We have a plan" isn't a plan. Cover AV failure, low turnout, venue changes, and dropouts explicitly, in writing.

04

Close the technical difficulty gap

The lowest-scoring metric in the study (2.7/5). Assign clear technical ownership and run a pre-event review.

05

Lead with Instagram, but don't rely on it alone

The most-used channel by far — pair it with WhatsApp and posters, and start the content calendar 3–4 weeks out.

06

Build one real handover document

Permissions workflow, vendor contacts, logistics timeline, authority hierarchy — archived on a shared drive, every year.
